Why Cross Entropy Loss is used instead of Mean Squared Error

Almost a year ago when I joined my current job, a question was asked in the interview that why simple mean squared error or euclidean distance isn't used despite its simplicity and why CE (cross entropy) became the standard first for multiple dependent variable classification problems then transformers. All I knew was the vanishing gradient problem.

I revisited the topic some time back and presenting you some fun math that is the result of AI and some tinkering with chain rules. Hope you enjoy!

Why We Can't Use MSE for Categorical Outputs

Mean Squared Error (MSE) is designed for regression tasks (predicting continuous numerical values like house prices, age, or temperature), whereas predicting disease and variety are classification tasks (predicting discrete categorical boundaries).

Here is why MSE doesn't work well for this setup:

  • Arbitrary Order and Distance: If we assign numerical values to categories (e.g. category_1 = 1, category_2 = 2, category_3 = 3), MSE assumes these relationships are ordinal and equidistant. Under MSE, a prediction of 3 for a true label of 1 is penalized heavily (difference of 2), while a prediction of 2 is penalized less (difference of 1). However, in classification, misclassifying a disease as normal is not "closer" or "better" than misclassifying it as dead_heart; all incorrect classes are equally incorrect.
  • Probabilistic Outputs (Softmax): In classification, our model outputs a probability distribution across categories using an activation function like Softmax. Cross-Entropy loss measures the difference between two probability distributions (the model's prediction vs. the one-hot encoded true label). It is highly sensitive to the confidence of the correct class prediction (providing heavy penalties for being confidently wrong), which drives the optimization process much more effectively than measuring the squared Euclidean distance with MSE.

Can We Just Use MSE on the Probabilities?

Yes, you can technically compute the MSE between the predicted probability vector and the one-hot encoded target vector (this is historically referred to as the Brier Score). However, we rarely use it to train deep learning classifiers due to a few critical issues with optimization:

Gradient Vanishing (The "Saturating" Problem)

When we use a Softmax activation at the output layer, the outputs are squashed between 0 and 1.

If we pair Softmax with Cross-Entropy, the derivative of the loss with respect to the pre-activation outputs (logits) simplifies beautifully to $(p_i - y_i)$. This means the gradient is directly proportional to the prediction error. Even if the model is confidently wrong, it gets a strong gradient signal to correct itself.

If we pair Softmax with MSE, the derivative includes the derivative of the Softmax function itself, which contains a term $p_i(1 - p_i)$. If the model predicts a probability very close to 0 or 1 (even if it's completely wrong, like predicting 0.0001 for the correct class), $p_i(1 - p_i)$ becomes extremely close to 0. This causes the gradient to vanish, meaning the model stops learning and cannot easily correct its confident mistakes.

Non-Convex Optimization Landscape

MSE applied to Softmax probabilities leads to a highly non-convex loss surface with many flat regions and local minima. Cross-Entropy provides a convex loss landscape relative to the logits, making optimization via gradient descent much faster and more reliable.

Penalization of Mistakes

Cross-Entropy penalizes extreme mistakes logarithmically. If the true label is 1 and the model predicts a probability of 0.001, Cross-Entropy loss goes to "infinity" ($-\log(0.001) \approx 6.9$), forcing the model to adjust. MSE would only penalize this mistake by $(1 - 0.001)^2 \approx 0.998$, which doesn't penalize confidently wrong predictions aggressively enough.

The Mathematics: Gradient of What Exactly?

When we train a neural network, backpropagation relies on the gradient of the loss ($L$) with respect to the logits ($z_i$)—which are the raw, unnormalized outputs of the last linear layer of the network before they are passed through the Softmax function ($p_i = \operatorname{softmax}(z_i)$).

Let's compare the gradients of the two approaches with respect to these logits ($z_i$) for a single training sample where class $k$ is the true class ($y_k = 1$, and $y_i = 0$ for $i \neq k$).

Softmax and Cross-Entropy Loss

The Cross-Entropy loss is:

$$L_{CE} = -\log(p_k)$$

Using the chain rule to find the gradient with respect to any logit $z_i$:

$$\frac{\partial L_{CE}}{\partial z_i} = p_i - y_i$$

What this means: The gradient is incredibly simple and clean. If the true label is $y_i = 1$ and our model predicts $p_i = 0.01$, the gradient is $0.01 - 1 = -0.99$. This is a large, strong gradient signal telling the network to aggressively adjust its weights to increase $p_i$.

Softmax + Mean Squared Error (MSE) Loss

If we treat the probabilities as targets, the MSE loss is:

$$L_{MSE} = \sum_j (p_j - y_j)^2$$

Using the chain rule to find the gradient with respect to logit $z_i$:

$$\frac{\partial L_{MSE}}{\partial z_i} = 2\sum_j (p_j - y_j)\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial z_i}$$

Since the derivative of Softmax is $\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial z_i} = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$ (where $\delta_{ij} = 1$ if $i = j$ and 0 otherwise), the final derivative simplifies to:

$$\frac{\partial L_{MSE}}{\partial z_i} = 2p_i\left((p_i - y_i) - \sum_j (p_j - y_j)p_j\right)$$

What this means: Notice the term $p_i$ multiplying the entire expression.

If the true label is $y_i = 1$ and our model confidently but wrongly predicts $p_i \approx 0.0001$, the leading multiplier $p_i$ is 0.0001.

Even though $(p_i - y_i) = -0.9999$ is a massive error, multiplying it by 0.0001 yields a final gradient that is extremely close to zero.

Because the gradient $\partial L/\partial z_i$ is virtually zero, the weights in the preceding layers of the network receive almost no update, leaving the model "stuck" in its incorrect configuration.

Connecting the Logit Gradient to the Weights

You are 100% correct: we do not update the logits $z$ directly during training; we update the network's weights ($w$).

However, the relationship between the loss, the logits, and the weights is linked explicitly by the Chain Rule of calculus. Let's look at how this connection works for any weight $w_{ij}$ in the final layer of the network.

The Explicit Link

The logit $z_i$ is computed from the incoming activations $a_j$ of the previous layer and the weights $w_{ij}$:

$$z_i = \sum_j w_{ij}a_j + b_i$$

Because of this linear relationship, the derivative of the logit with respect to its weight is simply the activation of the incoming neuron:

$$\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial w_{ij}} = a_j$$

Now, when we want to calculate the gradient of the Loss ($L$) with respect to the weight ($w_{ij}$) to perform our gradient descent update, we apply the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}\cdot a_j$$

Substituting the terms, we get,

$$\frac{\partial L_{CE}}{\partial w_{ij}} = (p_i - y_i)\cdot a_j$$

Why the Logit Gradient Dominates

This final equation shows that the gradient of the weights is directly proportional to the gradient of the logits:

With Cross-Entropy, we get the equation above, meanwhile with MSE, we get the following:

$$\frac{\partial L_{MSE}}{\partial w_{ij}} = 2p_i\left((p_i - y_i) - \sum_k (p_k - y_k)p_k\right)\cdot a_j$$

If the prediction error is large ($p_i - y_i \approx -1.0$), the weights receive a healthy, strong update proportional to the incoming activation $a_j$.

With MSE, the weight gradient is multiplied by $p_i$. If the model is confidently wrong ($p_i \approx 0$), the leading $p_i$ is 0. That zeroes the left hand side of the expression.

Summary

Even though we are updating the weights $w$, the gradient of the logits acts as a gatekeeper. If $\partial L/\partial z_i$ vanishes (which happens under MSE when the output saturates), then the gradient for all weights connected to that neuron ($\partial L/\partial w_{ij}$) also completely vanishes, regardless of how large the actual error is.
